# How to Play: Bretton Woods 1944

## Overview

**Bretton Woods 1944** is a multiplayer strategy game that recreates the historic 1944 conference where world leaders negotiated the post-war international economic order. Up to 7 players each represent a nation, competing to build the strongest economy from 1944 to 1952 through diplomacy, economic policy, and military strategy.

The game has two phases:
- **Phase 1 (The Conference):** Vote on 10 major international economic issues
- **Phase 2 (Post-War Era, 1946-1952):** Manage your nation's economy, military, and diplomacy across 7 years

The player with the highest combined score at the end wins.

---

## Getting Started

### 1. Create an Account
Enter a username and password on the login screen. Your session is saved automatically.

### 2. Join a Game
From the game list, join an available game room. Games show their current status (Lobby, Active, Phase 2, or Complete) and how many player slots remain.

### 3. Choose Your Country
In the lobby, select one of the 7 available nations. Each has unique strengths, weaknesses, and objectives. Once all players are ready, the host starts the game.

## The 7 Playable Nations

| Nation | Starting Position | Key Objectives |
|--------|------------------|----------------|
| **United States** | Creditor nation with 70% of world's gold reserves. Strongest economy and military. | Establish the dollar as the primary reserve currency. Ensure US dominance in international finance. |
| **United Kingdom** | Heavily indebted with a trade deficit and declining empire. | Preserve the Sterling bloc and Commonwealth preferences. Secure reconstruction loans. |
| **USSR** | War-devastated with a command economy. Suspicious of capitalist institutions. | Obtain reconstruction aid without conditions. Maintain economic sovereignty. |
| **France** | Occupied and devastated with a weak negotiating position. | Secure reconstruction financing. Restore economic stability and great power status. |
| **China** | Underdeveloped with civil war looming. Minimal international influence. | Gain international recognition. Secure development aid. |
| **India (British Raj)** | British colony in 1944-46, gains independence August 1947. | Represent Indian interests despite colonial rule. Prepare economic foundation for independence. |
| **Argentina** | Prosperous wartime neutral and creditor nation. Strong agriculture under Peron. | Protect agricultural exports. Lead the Latin American bloc. Develop industry through import substitution. |

### Special Nation Mechanics

- **USA** receives a trade balance bonus from being the reserve currency issuer.
- **USSR** and **China** (from 1949) use a **command economy** system with different policy controls (Five-Year Plans instead of market-based levers).
- **Argentina** has unique abilities: *Commodity Power* (influences grain/beef prices), *Third Position* (bonus points for policies differing from both USA and USSR), and *Latin American Leadership* (extra weight on development issues).

## Phase 1: The Conference (10 Rounds)

In each round, a major international economic issue is presented for a vote. Issues include:

1. Reserve Currency System
2. Exchange Rate System
3. Capital Controls
4. IMF Voting Power
5. Reconstruction Financing
6. Currency Stabilization
7. Trade Liberalization
8. Gold Standard vs. Bretton Woods
9. Soviet Participation
10. Post-War Economic Order

### How Voting Works

- Each issue presents 2-4 options (A, B, C, and sometimes D).
- The game shows which options **favor** or **oppose** your nation's interests.
- All players vote simultaneously. Once everyone has voted, results are revealed.
- If there's a tie, a **revote** is triggered (up to 2 revotes per issue).
- You earn points when options favorable to your country win, and lose points when opposed options win.

### Strategy Tips for Phase 1

- **Build coalitions.** Talk to other players and coordinate votes on issues that matter most to your nation.
- **Trade votes.** You might concede on an issue that barely affects you in exchange for support on one that's critical.
- **Watch the favors/opposes tags.** They show you exactly how each option impacts your score.

## Phase 2: Post-War Economic Management (1946-1952)

After the conference concludes, the game advances to Phase 2. You now manage your nation's economy and military year by year across 7 years.

### Each Year Includes:

1. **Review your economic dashboard** (GDP growth, inflation, unemployment, trade balance, gold reserves, exchange rate, industrial output)
2. **Set economic and military policy** using sliders
3. **Respond to crises** that arise from historical events or player actions
4. **Deploy troops** to regions around the world
5. **Resolve military conflicts** if forces clash

### Economic Policy (Market Economies)

If you play USA, UK, France, India, or Argentina, you set these policies each year:

| Policy | Range | What It Does |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| **Central Bank Interest Rate** | 0-10% | Lower rates boost growth but increase inflation. Higher rates control inflation but slow growth. Optimal around 3%. |
| **Exchange Rate** | 0.5-1.5 | Under Bretton Woods, rates are pegged. Devaluation (lower) makes exports competitive but imports expensive. |
| **Tariff Rate** | 0-50% | Protects domestic industry but tariffs above 15% hurt trade efficiency. Coordinating tariffs with other nations helps. |
| **Military Spending** | 0-25% of GDP | Higher spending builds military power but diverts resources from the economy. Balance is key. |

### Economic Policy (Command Economies: USSR, China from 1949)

| Policy | Range | What It Does |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| **Five-Year Plan Growth Target** | 3-15% | Higher targets drive rapid industrialization but risk bottlenecks and shortages. |
| **Heavy Industry Allocation** | 20-90% | More resources to heavy industry boosts industrial output but may neglect consumer goods. |
| **Foreign Trade Orientation** | 0-100 | 0 = trade only with COMECON bloc, 100 = trade with the West. |
| **Plan Fulfillment Priority** | 30-100 | How strictly GOSPLAN enforces targets. Higher priority = more output but less flexibility. |
| **Military Spending** | 0-25% of GDP | Same trade-off as market economies. |

### Military Forces

All nations also set the size of their three military branches each year:

- **Army** (land forces, in thousands)
- **Navy** (naval forces, in thousands)
- **Air Force** (air forces, in thousands)

Adjusting these within your military spending budget lets you project power in different ways. Naval strength is especially important for trade-dependent nations like the UK and USA.

---

## Troop Deployment

After setting policy, you can deploy troops to regions around the world. Deployments appear on the interactive map.

- If multiple nations deploy forces to the **same region**, a **tension alert** appears.
- At year's end, overlapping deployments can escalate into **military conflicts**.

---

## Military Conflicts

When forces from different nations occupy the same region, a battle phase begins.

### Declaring Diplomatic Stances

Before battle, you must declare your stance toward each other nation involved:
- **Allied** -- Full support (fight together)
- **Neutral** -- No interference
- **Enemy** -- Opposition

Alliances form automatically based on these declarations and affect battle outcomes.

### Battle Decisions

When conflict erupts, you choose one of three options:

| Decision | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| **Fight** | Maximize military advantage. Highest casualties but strongest chance of victory. |
| **Negotiate** | Seek a diplomatic resolution. Lower casualties, moderate outcomes. |
| **Retreat** | Preserve your forces. Minimal losses but you concede the region. |

### Battle Results

After all decisions are submitted, results show:
- The **winner** of the engagement
- **Casualties** for each nation
- The **outcome** (victory, stalemate, or defeat)
- What each participant decided (fight, negotiate, or retreat)

Military outcomes can trigger new crises with economic consequences.

---

## Crises

Throughout Phase 2, **historical crises** arise that demand a response. Some are triggered by the game timeline (e.g., the Berlin Blockade in 1948, the Korean War in 1950), while others are triggered by player military deployments.

- Each crisis presents **multiple response options** with different costs, military requirements, and economic effects.
- Your choices affect GDP growth, inflation, unemployment, and diplomatic standing.
- Handling crises well earns **diplomatic points**, which convert to final score points.

---

## The Economic Dashboard

Your dashboard displays key indicators updated each year:

| Indicator | What to Watch |
|-----------|--------------|
| **GDP Growth** | Positive growth = economic expansion. Aim for steady 3-5% growth. |
| **Inflation** | Keep below 3% for maximum score. Above 20% incurs penalties. |
| **Unemployment** | Below 2% = full employment bonus. Above 10% = penalty. |
| **Trade Balance** | Positive = trade surplus (good). Each surplus year earns 8 bonus points. |
| **Gold Reserves** | Below $500M triggers exchange rate instability. Above $15B boosts confidence. |
| **Exchange Rate** | Stable rates (<1% change) stay within the Bretton Woods band. Changes above 10% force devaluation. |
| **Industrial Output** | Index of your manufacturing capacity. Grows with investment and policy. |

### Exchange Rate Pressure Levels

| Change | Status |
|--------|--------|
| < 1% | Stable -- within Bretton Woods band |
| 1-5% | Pressure -- intervention required |
| 5-10% | Unstable -- market devaluation risk |
| > 10% | Crisis -- forced devaluation |

## Historical Events

Each year features real historical events that provide context and may influence gameplay:

- **1946:** Bretton Woods ratified, IMF and World Bank begin operations
- **1947:** Indian independence, Marshall Plan announced, Truman Doctrine
- **1948:** Berlin Blockade, State of Israel founded, OAS founded
- **1949:** NATO founded, Chinese Communist revolution, USSR tests atomic bomb
- **1950:** Korean War begins, European Payments Union created
- **1951:** Iranian oil crisis, Japanese Peace Treaty, ECSC treaty
- **1952:** Britain tests atomic bomb, Eisenhower elected, Korean War winds down

## Scoring

Your final score combines Phase 1 (conference voting) and Phase 2 (economic management).

### Phase 1 Scoring
- Points are awarded based on whether options favorable to your nation win the vote.
- Favored options winning = positive points; opposed options winning = negative points.

### Phase 2 Scoring (1947-1952)

| Category | How It's Scored | Max Points |
|----------|----------------|------------|
| **GDP Growth** | 10 points per % of average annual growth | Uncapped |
| **Inflation Control** | < 3% avg = 50 pts, < 5% = 40, < 10% = 25, < 20% = 10, 20%+ = -10 | 50 |
| **Employment** | < 2% avg unemployment = 40, < 4% = 30, < 6% = 15, < 10% = 5, 10%+ = -5 | 40 |
| **Trade Balance** | 8 points per year with a positive trade balance | 48 |
| **Economic Stability** | Low GDP variance = 30 pts, moderate = 15, high = 5 | 30 |
| **Bretton Woods Cooperation** | Bonus for voting with the majority on key economic issues in Phase 1 | Varies |
| **Crisis Diplomacy** | 2 points per diplomatic point earned from crisis responses | Varies |

### How to Win

The highest combined score across both phases wins. Medals are awarded:
- First place
- Second place
- Third place

## Quick Strategy Guide

### General Tips
- **Balance growth and stability.** Explosive GDP growth means nothing if inflation spirals out of control.
- **Keep unemployment low.** Full employment (< 2%) is a massive scoring bonus.
- **Maintain trade surpluses.** Each surplus year is worth 8 points -- consistency pays off.
- **Don't neglect the military.** Under-investing leaves you vulnerable, but over-investing starves your economy.
- **Respond to crises wisely.** Diplomatic points add up and convert directly to final score.

### Country-Specific Tips

- **USA:** You start with enormous economic advantages. Use your reserve currency bonus and gold reserves to dominate trade. Keep interest rates moderate.
- **UK:** Your trade deficit is your biggest challenge. Focus on rebuilding exports and securing favorable reconstruction terms. Watch the 1949 Sterling crisis.
- **USSR:** Your command economy gives you direct control over industrialization. Push ambitious Five-Year Plans but watch for bottlenecks. You're outside Bretton Woods, so exchange rates don't apply.
- **France:** You start weak but have room to grow. Prioritize reconstruction and stabilization. The 1948-49 franc devaluations will hit hard -- prepare for them.
- **China:** Hyperinflation (300%!) is your immediate crisis. Stabilize prices first, then focus on development. The 1949 Communist revolution changes your economy to command-based.
- **India:** Independence in 1947 is your turning point. Build a strong foundation early. Your trade surplus is an asset -- protect it.
- **Argentina:** Your commodity exports are powerful in the early years. Use the Third Position bonus by charting your own course between the USA and USSR. Watch for the post-1950 drought.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Bretton Woods System** | The real-world international monetary system (1944-1971) based on fixed exchange rates pegged to the US dollar, which was convertible to gold at $35/oz. |
| **IMF** | International Monetary Fund -- provides emergency loans to countries with balance-of-payments problems. |
| **World Bank** | International Bank for Reconstruction and Development -- finances post-war rebuilding and development projects. |
| **GATT** | General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade -- framework for reducing trade barriers. |
| **Marshall Plan** | US program providing $13 billion in aid to rebuild Western European economies (1948-1952). |
| **Command Economy** | Economic system where the state controls production, prices, and trade (USSR, China from 1949). |
| **GOSPLAN** | Soviet State Planning Committee that set production targets for the entire economy. |
| **Five-Year Plan** | Soviet economic planning cycle with ambitious industrialization targets. |
| **Third Position** | Argentina's Peronist foreign policy doctrine -- neither aligned with US capitalism nor Soviet communism. |
| **Exchange Rate Peg** | Fixing a currency's value relative to the US dollar within a narrow band. |
| **Devaluation** | Officially reducing a currency's exchange rate, making exports cheaper and imports more expensive. |
| **Trade Balance** | The difference between a nation's exports and imports. Positive = surplus, negative = deficit. |
| **Gold Reserves** | A nation's holdings of gold, backing currency stability under Bretton Woods. |
