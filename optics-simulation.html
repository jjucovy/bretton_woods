Core Optics Principles

The Thin Lens Equation

All image formation by a single lens obeys:

1/f = 1/do + 1/di

Where f is focal length, do is object distance, and di is image distance. A positive image distance means a real image forms on the far side; negative means virtual.

Lateral Magnification

The size ratio of image to object:

m = −di / do = hi / ho

A negative m means the image is inverted. For a telescope objective aimed at a distant object, do ≈ ∞, so the intermediate image forms at exactly the focal point.

Telescope Magnification

For parallel light from infinity, total angular magnification is simply:

Mtel = fobj / feye

Longer objective focal lengths and shorter eyepiece focal lengths both increase power. The image is inverted — astronomers often accept this, but terrestrial scopes add an erecting lens system.

Compound Microscope Magnification

Total power is the product of both stages:

Mmic = mobj × Meye = (L / fobj) × (250 / feye)

L is the optical tube length (≈ 160 mm standard). The 250 mm is the conventional near-point distance of the human eye used to define eyepiece magnification.

Resolving Power (Telescopes)

The Rayleigh criterion for angular resolution:

θ = 1.22 λ / D (radians)

In arcseconds: θ″ ≈ 116 / Dmm. A larger aperture D resolves finer detail. This is why professional telescopes have enormous mirrors — not just for light gathering but for resolution.

Resolving Power (Microscopes)

Abbe's diffraction limit:

d = λ / (2 · NA) = λ / (2 · n · sin α)

NA is numerical aperture. Higher NA (oil immersion, n ≈ 1.515) and shorter wavelengths push resolution below 200 nm. This is why electron microscopes use electrons (λ ≈ pm) for atomic-scale imaging.

Light Gathering Power

A telescope collects light proportional to its area:

LGP = (D / Deye)² ≈ (D / 7mm)²

A 100 mm scope collects ~200× more light than a dark-adapted eye (7 mm pupil), revealing objects far too faint to see unaided. This is the primary driver for large observatory apertures.

Real vs. Virtual Images

A real image forms where light rays actually converge — it can be projected onto a screen. A virtual image appears where rays seem to diverge from — it cannot be screened but your eye can focus on it as if it were a real object. Eyepieces always produce virtual final images.

Numerical Aperture & f/ratio

Telescope focal ratio:

f/# = fobj / D

Slow scopes (f/10+) give wider exit pupils and better eye relief. Fast scopes (f/4–f/6) are physically shorter and suit wide-field viewing, but demand better-quality eyepieces to control aberrations.
